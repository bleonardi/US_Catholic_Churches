Canonical Rigidity and the Misallocation of Sacred Space

Girard, Lösch, and the Territorial Logic of the American Catholic Church

Author
Affiliation

Benedict Leonardi

University of Cincinnati, Department of Economics

Published

July 17, 2026

Abstract

The Roman Catholic Church operates a national territorial infrastructure — approximately 23,000 parishes — whose boundaries were drawn for a pedestrian, high-adherence 19th century. We argue that this infrastructure exhibits two interlocking pathologies. The first is economic: canonical boundaries diverge from the Löschian natural market partition, producing measurable spatial leakage (the Canonical Leakage Index) and chronic asset underutilization. The second is anthropological: following Girard, the diocesan institution responds to territorial crisis not by reorganizing the map but by sacrificing individual parishes — designating pharmakoi whose closure restores apparent order without touching the underlying misallocation. We trace both pathologies through a national dataset of 23,000 geolocated parishes, two deep-dive dioceses (Detroit and Cincinnati), and 26 years of sacramental panel data. Detroit, in full crisis, illustrates the complete Girardian sequence: mimetic undifferentiation → sacrificial selection → spatial void. Cincinnati, earlier in the same arc, provides the pre-crisis control, validating the structural pattern while isolating the AOD amplifier specific to Detroit. Together they establish that the institution preserves its canonical map by consuming its weakest nodes — and that the cost of that preservation is borne spatially, by the communities that surround the sacrifice.

I. Introduction

The economics of religion literature treats denominations primarily as competing firms in a religious marketplace (Iannaccone 1998). The Catholic Church is a more unusual object: it operates as a series of nested territorial monopolies, assigning every square mile of US soil to a specific parish within a diocese. In theory, this grants each parish a captive geographic market. In practice, the territorial assignments were calibrated for a pedestrian-accessible, high-adherence era, and two macroeconomic shifts have eroded that calibration. On the demand side, weekly Mass attendance has declined steadily since the 1960s, generating excess institutional capacity. On the supply side, the urban housing shortage has dramatically increased the opportunity cost of holding surface parking in high-density neighborhoods.

The economic framing captures what the Church is trying to do — allocate ritual services across space — but not why it consistently fails to adapt the map through which it does so. That question requires an anthropological supplement. We argue, following Girard (1972), that the persistence of canonical rigidity is not a coordination failure. It is a sacrificial logic: when the territorial order that constitutes the diocese as an institution is threatened by demographic change, the institution responds not by reorganizing the order but by consuming its weakest nodes. The parish that is closed is the pharmakos — the scapegoat whose expulsion temporarily restores coherence to a system whose underlying misallocation is left intact.

This paper layers Girard onto Lösch. The Löschian framework (Lösch 1954) tells us what efficient allocation of sacred services across space would look like and measures the gap between that ideal and the canonical reality. The Girardian framework tells us why the institution will not close that gap through internal reform alone, and what the sacrifice costs the communities that absorb the void. The paper proceeds through four movements: theoretical (§II), historical (§III), cross-sectional (§§IV–V), and longitudinal (§§VI–VIII), culminating in a land-economics assessment of what the sacrifice leaves behind (§IX).

II. Theoretical Framework

IIa. The Löschian Parish: Hotelling, Huff, and the Canonical Leakage Index

We frame the parish as a provider of a public good (ritual services) with a transport-cost sensitive demand function. Following Hotelling (1929), a rational parishioner \(j\) minimizes:

\[C_{ij} = P_i + t \cdot d_{ij}\]

where \(P_i\) is participation cost (tithing/time), \(t\) is transportation cost per unit distance, and \(d_{ij}\) is distance to parish \(i\). In a frictionless market, parish boundaries would form a Voronoi tessellation — each parishioner assigned to their nearest parish. Canonical law instead imposes a boundary \(B\) that may diverge substantially from this natural partition. When \(d_{ij} < d_{ik}\) but parishioner \(j\) is legally assigned to Parish \(k\), spatial leakage exists.

A more realistic formulation treats attendance probability as a continuous gravity function (Huff 1963):

\[P_{ij} = \frac{S_i^\alpha / d_{ij}^\beta}{\sum_k S_k^\alpha / d_{kj}^\beta}\]

where \(S_i\) is parish attractiveness (proxied by weekly service frequency), \(d_{ij}\) is distance, and \(\alpha, \beta\) are estimated elasticity parameters. This generates a continuous leakage surface.

The Canonical Leakage Index (CLI) for parish \(i\) is:

\[\text{CLI}_i = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j \in B_i} P_{ij} \cdot \text{pop}_j}{\sum_{j \in B_i} \text{pop}_j}\]

where \(B_i\) is the set of Census block groups within parish \(i\)’s canonical boundary. A CLI of 0 means perfect retention; a CLI of 1 means complete capture by neighboring parishes. Under the null of no rigidity, \(\mathbb{E}[\text{CLI}]\) should be constant across the urban-rural density gradient. The observed pattern — CLI rising sharply with urban density — is the cross-sectional signature of canonical rigidity.

IIb. The Girardian Diocese: Mimetic Crisis and the Pharmakos

Girard (1972) argues that social order is constituted through differentiation — the drawing of distinctions that prevent the undifferentiated mimetic rivalry he calls the “crisis of differences.” Sacred boundaries are the primary instrument of that differentiation. In the Catholic diocesan context, canonical territorial boundaries are not merely administrative lines: they are the spatial expression of a differentiating order that assigns every Catholic body to a specific sacred jurisdiction, distinguishing each parish from its neighbors through the particularity of territory, history, and community.

This differentiating order was stable so long as the parish’s canonical boundary and its social community were roughly coextensive — which they were in the pedestrian city of the early 20th century. The parish was the neighborhood; the neighborhood was the parish; the boundary between them was both canonical and social. Postwar automobile mobility dissolved this coextension without dissolving the canonical map. Populations migrated outward; boundaries did not. The canonical distinctions that maintained order now float free of the social reality they were meant to organize. This is what Girard would recognize as mimetic undifferentiation: the boundaries are still drawn, but the differences they mark have ceased to correspond to anything outside themselves.

The institution’s characteristic response to a crisis of differences is the scapegoat mechanism (Girard 1986). Rather than reorganize the differentiating order — which would require acknowledging that the order itself is contingent — the institution designates a pharmakos whose expulsion restores apparent coherence. The scapegoat is selected not because it is the cause of the crisis, but because it is sufficiently marginal and sufficiently distinguishable to serve as its symbolic container. In the diocesan restructuring process, the pharmakos is the parish marked for closure. It is selected on attendance and financial metrics — measures of its own weakness — while the canonical map that produced its weakness is left untouched. Each closure is announced as a rational response to demographic reality. What it actually accomplishes is the preservation of the territorial fiction by consuming one of its weakest nodes.

The empirical implication is testable: if the diocese operates sacrificial logic, then high-CLI parishes — those whose canonical boundaries have become most fictive, most divorced from their actual social catchment — should face elevated closure risk, controlling for their absolute scale of decline. The misallocation itself should predict the sacrifice.

III. Historical Origins: The Making of the Misallocation

The CLI framework measures the current state of canonical rigidity but does not explain why the misallocation is deepest in the metros where it is most entrenched. That explanation requires a historical lens. Suburbanization is the triggering event of the mimetic crisis: the moment when the pedestrian map of the early 20th century stopped matching the automobile geography that replaced it. The congregation-trend data show that high-CLI metros are precisely those with the deepest pre-suburban Catholic institutional roots — cities where the parish network was so dense before the postwar wave that the institution never fully relocated outward with its membership. The Q5 metros (Boston, Chicago, Detroit, Milwaukee, Providence) had the most differentiated pre-suburban parish geography to lose, which is why the mimetic crisis is deepest there.

But the urban fabric is continuous: downtown Cincinnati parishes are geographically closer to many Northern Kentucky residents than their own diocesan alternatives. Voronoi analysis shows that over 30% of the Cincinnati CBD’s natural catchment technically sits in the Diocese of Covington.

VIIb. Philadelphia: A Third Girardian Case

Philadelphia is the oldest Catholic diocese in the United States (established 1808), with parishes dating to 1733 — a colonial-era depth absent from Detroit and Cincinnati. Its crisis trigger differs from the AOD collapse: the Archdiocese of Philadelphia faced cascading financial pressure from clergy abuse settlements culminating in the 2011 grand jury report, prompting the Project HOME restructuring that began closing and merging parishes in 2012. The Girardian sequence is the same — a crisis, a deliberative process, a sacrificial selection — but the pharmakos-selection mechanism is distinct, driven by financial shock rather than opioid-era population collapse. Philadelphia thus provides a stress test of the framework’s generalizability across crisis types.

VIIb-iv. Girardian Interpretation

Project HOME designated 49 parishes for closure or merger beginning in 2012. The selection criteria — attendance levels, financial viability, building condition — mirror those applied in the Detroit AOD restructuring: the pharmakos is again the post-suburban peripheral parish, not the canonical map. Philadelphia’s pre-industrial parishes (Old St. Joseph’s, Old St. Mary’s, St. Augustine’s) were spared; the sacrifice concentrated on the outer ring despite that ring holding the smallest share of the total parish population. The mechanism differs from Detroit — financial shock rather than opioid population collapse — but the sacrificial logic is identical: crisis triggers institutional deliberation, deliberation selects peripheral parishes as the offering. Philadelphia thus confirms that the Girardian sequence generalizes across crisis types, and that the canonical map’s rigidity produces the same misallocation whether the crisis originates in demographics or finance.

X. Conclusion

The canonical map is not simply outdated — it is actively maintained by a sacrificial logic that consumes individual parishes rather than reorganizing territorial boundaries. The Löschian framework tells us what efficient allocation of sacred services across space would look like, and measures a gap between that ideal and the canonical reality that is largest precisely in the metros where Catholicism was strongest. The Girardian framework tells us why that gap will not be closed through internal reform alone: the institution cannot acknowledge that the map is the problem without dissolving the differentiating order that gives the map its sacred authority. Each parish closure is not a rationalization — it is a sacrifice that preserves the fiction of the map by consuming one of its weakest nodes.

Cincinnati and Detroit together constitute a natural experiment in the timing of institutional sacrifice. Detroit has passed through the full sequence: misallocation → mimetic undifferentiation → sacrifice → void. Cincinnati is where Detroit was in approximately 2005: the same structural pattern, the same cohort gradient, the same null interaction between founding lag and rate of decline — but the restructuring process has not yet been triggered. The comparison isolates the AOD crisis as the amplifier that converts structural rigidity into institutional emergency, rather than the cause of the rigidity itself.

The structural finding — founding lag determines the level of sacramental activity, not the rate of decline — has a precise policy interpretation. The institution cannot accelerate its way out of this by making individual parishes more vital. The level effect is set by where the parish was founded relative to the demographic frontier; no amount of pastoral renewal changes the founding year. Reform requires either external demographic pressure sufficient to force territorial reorganization at scale, or a theological reframing that decouples canonical territory from sacred identity — that makes the map a convenience rather than a constitution.
