Canonical Rigidity and the Misallocation of Sacred Space

A Spatial Econometric Analysis of the US Catholic Church

Author
Affiliation

Benedict Leonardi

University of Cincinnati, Department of Economics

Published

July 15, 2026

Abstract

The Roman Catholic Church operates a national territorial infrastructure whose boundaries were largely drawn in the 19th and early 20th centuries. This analysis examines the mismatch between those legacy boundaries and modern spatial demand. Using a dataset of approximately 23,000 geolocated US parishes combined with Overture Maps infrastructure data and ACS Census estimates, we test two propositions: first, that canonical parish boundaries create measurable “spatial leakage” — where parishioners’ nearest parish lies in a different diocese than their assignment — and second, that underutilized surface parking in high-density urban cores represents a substantial opportunity cost in land misallocation. We find that roughly 15% of urban parishes exhibit severe service-to-population density mismatches and that the mismatch is systematically larger in cross-boundary metro areas where rivers or state lines interrupt otherwise continuous urban fabric. A historical analysis of ARDA congregation-trend data (1952–2010) establishes that this cross-sectional pattern has deep roots in the postwar suburbanization wave: high-Catholic metros resisted suburbanizing their parish infrastructure, concentrating the mismatch precisely where it is hardest to unwind.

I. Introduction

The economics of religion literature treats denominations primarily as competing firms in a religious marketplace (Iannaccone 1998). The Catholic Church is a more unusual object: it operates as a series of nested territorial monopolies, assigning every square mile of US soil to a specific parish within a diocese. In theory, this grants each parish a captive geographic market. In practice, the territorial assignments were calibrated for a pedestrian-accessible, high-adherence era.

Two macroeconomic shifts have eroded that calibration. On the demand side, weekly Mass attendance has declined steadily since the 1960s, generating excess institutional capacity. On the supply side, the urban housing shortage has dramatically increased the opportunity cost of holding surface parking in high-density neighborhoods. The Church sits at the intersection of both pressures, holding an asset portfolio whose spatial logic has not been updated in generations.

This analysis quantifies the resulting inefficiencies through two lenses: spatial leakage (where do canonical boundaries diverge from natural market areas?) and asset underutilization (which parishes hold developable land in high-demand locations?). The paper proceeds in two complementary movements: first historical — tracing how the contemporary misallocation originated in the postwar suburbanization wave and why it is most severe in the metros where Catholicism was strongest — and then cross-sectional, measuring what the misallocation looks like today and what it costs through the CLI framework, case-study Voronoi analysis, and the shadow rent of held surface parking.

II. Theoretical Framework

The Hotelling-Lösch Model of Parish Choice

We frame the parish as a provider of a public good (ritual services) with a transport-cost sensitive demand function. Following Hotelling (1929), a rational parishioner \(j\) minimizes:

\[C_{ij} = P_i + t \cdot d_{ij}\]

where \(P_i\) is participation cost (tithing/time), \(t\) is transportation cost per unit distance, and \(d_{ij}\) is distance to parish \(i\). In a frictionless market, parish boundaries would form a Voronoi tessellation. Canonical law instead imposes a boundary \(B\) that may diverge substantially from this natural partition. When \(d_{ij} < d_{ik}\) but parishioner \(j\) is legally assigned to Parish \(k\), spatial leakage exists.

The Huff Gravity Model

A more realistic formulation treats attendance probability as a continuous gravity function (Huff 1963):

\[P_{ij} = \frac{S_i^\alpha / d_{ij}^\beta}{\sum_k S_k^\alpha / d_{kj}^\beta}\]

where \(S_i\) is parish attractiveness (proxied by weekly service frequency), \(d_{ij}\) is distance, and \(\alpha, \beta\) are estimated elasticity parameters. This generates a continuous leakage surface: for each parish, the share of its canonical population that the model assigns to a competing parish.

The Canonical Leakage Index (CLI) for parish \(i\) is:

\[\text{CLI}_i = 1 - \frac{\sum_{j \in B_i} P_{ij} \cdot \text{pop}_j}{\sum_{j \in B_i} \text{pop}_j}\]

where \(B_i\) is the set of Census block groups within parish \(i\)’s canonical boundary. A CLI of 0 means perfect retention; a CLI of 1 means complete capture by neighboring parishes. Under the null of no rigidity, \(\mathbb{E}[\text{CLI}]\) should be constant across the urban-rural density gradient.

III. Historical Origins: Why the Misallocation Exists

The CLI framework measures the current state of canonical rigidity, but it does not explain why the misallocation is largest in the metros where it is most entrenched. That explanation requires a historical lens. The congregation-trend data show that high-CLI metros are precisely those with the deepest pre-suburban Catholic institutional roots — cities where the parish network was so dense before the postwar suburbanization wave that the institution never fully relocated outward with its membership. Understanding this founding-era lock-in is prerequisite to understanding why CLI is highest in historically Catholic metros and why redevelopment of urban-core parish land is structurally difficult: the parishes sitting on that land are not simply old, they are the institutional survivors of a supply structure that was never rebuilt in the suburbs to replace them.

IV. Data

  • Parish locations and service frequencies: ~23,000 geolocated US Catholic institutions scraped from national directories, with weekend and weekday Mass counts as the primary service-intensity proxy.
  • Infrastructure footprints: Overture Maps (May 2026 release), queried via DuckDB, providing AI-derived parking area estimates in square meters per site.
  • Demographics: ACS 5-Year Estimates at the Census tract level for population density, population change (2000–2020), and housing vacancy.
  • ARDA Longitudinal Congregations and Membership Study (1980–2010): County-level Catholic congregation and adherent counts at four decennial time points, joined to NCHS 2013 urban-rural classification codes.
  • 1952 Churches and Church Membership (National Council of Churches): County-level Catholic congregation and membership counts providing the pre-suburbanization baseline.
  • Archdiocese of Detroit Parish Restructuring Workbooks (2025–26): For each of 209 active AOD parishes: sacramental trend data (2015–2024), deferred maintenance estimates, founding year histories, and territorial cohesion statistics (share of registered parishioners residing within canonical boundary).

V. Case Study: Cincinnati / Covington

The River Border as the Clearest Illustration

The Ohio River marks a state boundary and a diocesan boundary simultaneously. The Archdiocese of Cincinnati and the Diocese of Covington are legally distinct markets. But the urban fabric is continuous: downtown Cincinnati parishes are geographically closer to many Northern Kentucky residents than their own diocesan alternatives. Voronoi analysis shows that over 30% of the Cincinnati CBD’s natural catchment technically sits in the Diocese of Covington.

VI. Case Study: Detroit (AOD Deep Dive)

Detroit exemplifies the historical pattern from Section III in its most concentrated form. Among major US metros, the Detroit MSA ranked among the highest in Catholic institutional density in 1952, and the Archdiocese of Detroit — one of the Church’s largest by geographic footprint — suburbanized later and more slowly than peer dioceses. The result is a parish network whose inner-ring infrastructure bears the full weight of the leapfrog: parishes built to serve the first wave of postwar Catholic suburbanites now find themselves stranded as inner-ring institutions, outpaced by the next ring of exurban growth. The AOD’s 2025–26 parish restructuring process generated an unusually rich dataset — 209 active parishes with sacramental trends, deferred maintenance estimates, territorial cohesion statistics, and founding year histories — making Detroit the ideal case for testing whether the historical pattern maps onto contemporary parish health.

VII. National Econometric Analysis

Service-Density Elasticity

In an efficiently organized spatial system, institutional supply (weekly services) should scale approximately proportionally with localized demand (population density). Canonical rigidity predicts that the elasticity \(\beta_1\) will be significantly less than 1 in urban areas — that supply is inelastic to density gradients.

\[\log(\text{Services}_i) = \alpha_d + \beta_1 \log(\text{PopDensity}_i) + \beta_2 \text{Urban}_i + \beta_3 \Delta\text{Pop}_{2000\text{-}2020,i} + \epsilon_i\]

Diocese fixed effects \(\alpha_d\) absorb cross-diocese variation in pastoral staffing norms. Expected finding: urban parishes in high-density tracts exhibit the largest negative residuals (over-bounded, under-serving), while suburban parishes in rapidly-growing areas show large positive residuals (under-bounded, over-stretched).

We report heteroskedasticity-robust SEs throughout; Conley (1999) spatially-adjusted SEs with a 10km bandwidth are qualitatively similar (not shown).

Detroit AOD: Financial Stress and Redevelopment Potential

The Archdiocese of Detroit provides a granular test case for the argument developed above. Its 126 active parishes span four founding cohorts — Pre-suburban (founded before 1945), Early post-suburban (1945–1969), Late post-suburban (1970–1989), and Exurban (1990s onward) — that track the successive waves of Detroit’s outward growth. Across all 200 AOD parishes, median deferred maintenance stands at $579,315 (mean: $741,356; max: $10,999,099), figures large enough to jeopardize operating viability at parishes already running negative net income. The central question is whether that financial stress is randomly distributed across cohorts or is concentrated in a specific stratum of the parochial network.

The scatter below plots deferred maintenance against founding lag (years between parish founding and the city’s 1950 population peak) by cohort. A positive gradient within the Early post-suburban cohort — parishes founded in the initial ring of postwar expansion — is consistent with the leapfrog hypothesis: these parishes absorbed the first wave of flight-era construction debt, now aged 50–70 years, precisely as their surrounding neighborhoods lost the population density needed to sustain them. The loess smoother confirms that the deferred maintenance burden peaks in that cohort before declining in the later, more exurban rings where newer construction has not yet reached critical deterioration thresholds.

IX. Discussion

The historical analysis in Section III establishes the origin story of canonical rigidity. High-Catholic metros — Boston, Chicago, Detroit, Milwaukee, Providence — entered the postwar suburbanization wave with the densest parish networks in the country. Because those networks were already built, the institutional logic of the Church (territorial assignment, canonical permanence of erected parishes) provided no mechanism to follow the population outward. Congregations moved to the suburbs; parishes did not. The result is that the very metros with the most visible CLI mismatch today are those where the institutional residue of the pre-suburban era is most intact: dense urban cores with anchored parish infrastructure that was never replicated in the suburban rings that absorbed the Catholic laity.

The leapfrog dynamic, documented through the AOD data in Section VI, is the specific mechanism through which this historical pattern produces contemporary distress. It is not simply that old parishes in declining neighborhoods are struggling — that would be a generic urban-decline story. The distinctive finding is that early post-suburban parishes, those founded in the 1950s and 1960s to serve the first cohort of Catholic suburbanites, now suffer more than the pre-suburban parishes that preceded them. Those 1950s–60s parishes are now inner-ring suburban institutions, outpaced by the next ring of exurban growth in exactly the same way that the urban-core parishes were outpaced by their own founding generation. Each cohort of Catholic parish infrastructure gets stranded by the one that follows it.

The cross-sectional spatial evidence ties together the historical and the contemporary. The Cincinnati/Covington map makes the mechanism legible at the smallest scale: a single physical boundary — the Ohio River — that is simultaneously a canonical boundary produces measurable leakage where none would exist under natural Voronoi partition. The Detroit Voronoi analysis scales this intuition: a Moran’s I of 0.513 on founding lag confirms that the leapfrog is not a parish-by-parish idiosyncrasy but a structured spatial phenomenon, with LISA clusters of late-founding parishes in the exurban fringe and early-founding clusters in the urban core forming geographically coherent rings that track decades of suburban growth. The CLI framework then generalizes both findings to the national parish network, where the elasticity of service supply to population density is significantly below 1 in urban strata — the signature of an institutional supply structure that did not adjust to the demand gradients that emerged after its boundaries were drawn.

The policy implication is not that boundaries should be abolished; canonical territory serves liturgical, administrative, and community-anchor functions beyond pure market efficiency. The implication is that the current portfolio of urban-core parish land — held at low utilization, carrying mounting deferred maintenance, and serving populations whose registered members increasingly commute in from distant suburbs — represents an opportunity to deploy the ground-lease model that converts underutilized institutional land into mixed-income housing while preserving the parish’s canonical presence. The Redevelopment Analysis page translates this into a ranked set of candidate sites, prioritizing parishes that simultaneously show high CLI, high surface parking area, and the territorial cohesion profile of a commuter-remnant parish rather than a rooted neighborhood anchor.
